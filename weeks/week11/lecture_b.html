What you will use from Tuesday

\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)=\frac{\tau^2}{n}+\frac{\sigma^2}{n\,r},\qquad \text{design effect}=1+(r-1)\rho,\qquad \rho=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}

\operatorname{Var}(\hat D)=\frac{1}{n}\Bigl(\tau_d^2+\frac{\sigma_{70}^2}{r_{70}}+\frac{\sigma_{8}^2}{r_{8}}\Bigr)\qquad\text{(two models on the same problems)}

  • n problems, r answers to each; \tau^2 between, \sigma^2 within.
  • Calls fixed: more problems win.
  • Two prices: the cheaper, more variable model gets more answers.

About 3 minutes. \tau_d^2 is the between-problem variance of the difference in accuracy. Remind students that the cost rule r^\ast from Tuesday needs a budget that counts problems too; the lab’s budget counts only calls.

How the lab runs

  1. One problem the model gets right half the time, then all 127.
  2. Ten answers each: the problem as the unit, and the design effect.
  3. Between and within variance, and how often two answers disagree.
  4. Spend 1,200 calls: rank four designs, then simulate them.
  5. If there is time: 200 paid calls on the 70B model.

A planning sheet for any evaluation

Plan item Ask
Objective What decision, and what result would change it?
Response What counts as right, and who decides?
Treatments What are you comparing?
Held constant What stays fixed: temperature, prompt, model version?
Nuisance factors What else varies? Block it, randomize it, or measure it.
Experimental unit What is one case? A new client case is one more of these.
Restrictions and budget What costs money: cases, answers, or labels?
Analysis Which comparison and interval, written before the first call?

Adapted from Coleman and Montgomery’s (1993) pre-design guide sheet for planned experiments. Replication, randomization, and blocking are Fisher’s (1935) three principles.

About 3 minutes. Coleman, D. E., and Montgomery, D. C. (1993), “A Systematic Approach to Planning for a Designed Industrial Experiment,” Technometrics 35(1), 1–12. Their Master Guide Sheet lists objectives, background, response variables, control variables (what we call treatments), factors to be held constant, nuisance factors, interactions, restrictions, design preferences including blocking and randomization, analysis, and trial runs. Their rule for nuisance factors: if its level can be set for each unit, block on it or randomize it; if not (it varies unpredictably), measure it and use it as a covariate. For LLM evaluations: block on the question; randomize call order, seeds, and which response is shown first; measure things like response length. Fisher, R. A. (1935), The Design of Experiments. Examples for each row: in Lab 11 the unit is a problem and the treatments are the two models; in the project, a new client photo, Reddit pair, or prompt is one more case, and related cases (repeated photos, pairs from one post, two responses to one prompt) are resampled together (Week 4B). The response is Week 2’s measurement question; the budget is Tuesday’s Question 3; the analysis written first is Week 5’s margin and Week 10’s flip rule.

The steps of an experiment

  1. State the problem: the client’s decision, and the result that would change it.
  2. Choose the factors: the treatments, what is held constant, the nuisance factors.
  3. Choose the response: what counts as right.
  4. Choose the design: the unit, the blocks, the randomization, n and r.
  5. Run it, as planned.
  6. Analyze it as it was designed, resampling the unit.
  7. Conclude and recommend.

The steps of experimentation in Coleman and Montgomery (1993), Table 1. For step 4: “Block what you can; randomize what you cannot,” attributed to George Box.

About 3 minutes. Coleman and Montgomery’s Table 1 lists the steps as recognition and statement of the problem, choice of factors and levels, selection of the response variables, choice of experimental design, conduction of the experiment, data analysis, and conclusions and recommendations; the first three are the planning phase. For step 4, a small pilot (say 20 questions with 5 answers each) gives a rough \tau^2 and \sigma^2, enough to pick r because the SE curve is flat near its minimum (Tuesday). Box’s mantra is quoted widely; Pashley and Miratrix (2020) discuss when blocking pays. Ask the room to run the steps for one track in a minute: for Track 3, the decision is whether the judge can stand in for human preferences; the unit is a pair, grouped by Reddit post; the judge saw both orders, so order is crossed with the pair; the answers are deterministic, so replication is across pairs and posts; and the comparison and its interval are written before the holdout is opened.
