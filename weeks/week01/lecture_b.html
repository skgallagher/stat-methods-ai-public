Fitting means searching

Logistic regression and a neural network both minimize the same loss: the negative log-likelihood, which ML calls binary cross-entropy. With features, there is no formula for the answer like \hat p = k/n. We search for the minimum.

At each guess, the derivative of the loss, the slope of the tangent line, says which way is downhill and how steep it is.

Two ways to search

  • Gradient descent, the ML default: take a small step downhill, sized by the slope. Repeat. \;\beta_{\text{new}} = \beta - \eta\, L'(\beta)
  • Newton–Raphson, the statistics default: use the slope and the curvature to jump to the bottom of the local parabola. \;\beta_{\text{new}} = \beta - L'(\beta)/L''(\beta)

Both start at 0, the usual default. Newton is fast but can overshoot from a bad start: from β₀ = −2 on this problem it jumps past the minimum and diverges. Software adds safeguards such as step halving. Gradient descent is slow but cheap per step, which matters when there are millions of weights.

Converged, or close enough?

Statistics Machine learning
Typical method Newton–Raphson (what glm and statsmodels use) gradient descent on random batches of data
Stops when the estimate stops changing held-out error stops improving, or time runs out
Ends at the MLE close to a minimum, not exactly the MLE
Standard errors from the curvature at the MLE not routinely computed

Statistics goes all the way to the optimum, because the theory behind standard errors and tests lives there. ML stops when it is close enough. With millions of weights the exact minimum is out of reach, and stopping early keeps a network from fitting noise.

Curvature gives the standard error

A sharply curved log-likelihood pins the estimate down. A flat one does not.

\operatorname{SE}(\hat\theta) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\ell''(\hat\theta)}}.

Check with p. At \hat p = k/n, -\ell''(\hat p) = n/\{\hat p(1-\hat p)\}, so \operatorname{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}: the formula you already know. This is where the standard errors in summary() come from.

How precise is a function of \hat p?

Curvature gave us the precision of \hat p:

\operatorname{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}.

But we often report a function of \hat p, such as Tuesday’s odds \hat p/(1-\hat p), or the log-odds \operatorname{logit}(\hat p), which is the logistic regression intercept \hat\beta_0.

How precise are those?

The idea: zoom in until the curve is a line

The same tangent-line idea we used to fit. Near \hat p, the curve is almost its tangent line, and the tangent stretches the spread of \hat p by the derivative g'(\hat p):

\operatorname{SE}\{g(\hat p)\} \approx |g'(\hat p)|\;\operatorname{SE}(\hat p).

This is the delta method.

The formal version expands around the true p: g(p̂) ≈ g(p) + g′(p)(p̂ − p), so Var{g(p̂)} ≈ g′(p)² p(1 − p)/n. In practice we plug in p̂.

Worked example: the odds

With 70 animal photos out of 100, \hat p = 0.7 and \operatorname{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{0.7 \times 0.3}{100}} = 0.046.

  1. Estimate. The odds are 0.7/0.3 = 2.33, about 7 animal photos for every 3 empty ones.
  2. Derivative. For g(p) = p/(1-p), g'(p) = 1/(1-p)^2. The tangent to the curve at \hat p = 0.7 has g'(0.7) = 1/0.3^2 = 11.1.
  3. Standard error. Multiply the derivative by \operatorname{SE}(\hat p): 11.1 \times 0.046 = 0.51.

In HW1 you do the same for the intercept \hat\beta_0 = \operatorname{logit}(\hat p).

Three reference points

An AI accuracy of 90% means little until you know what simpler approaches get. In the lab we compare three things on the same photos:

  1. Always guess the more common label. If half the photos are empty, always saying “empty” is right half the time.
  2. A simple model. Logistic regression on brightness, color, and edges.
  3. The AI. MegaDetector’s probabilities, turned into calls with a cutoff.

Which mistakes should we look at?

Looking at errors is how you learn what a model gets wrong. But if you browse until something looks interesting, you will find a story every time.

  • Pick the errors by a rule set in advance: one random error and the most confident error for each model.
  • What you see can suggest why a model fails.
  • Four hand-picked errors cannot tell you how common that failure is.

An extra model, after the fact

In HW1 you try one bigger change with AI help, such as a nonlinear model or a new feature. Suppose the simple model gets 0.78 on the held-out cameras. After seeing that, an AI suggests a random forest, which gets 0.84 on the same cameras.

  • It is worth reporting, as exploration.
  • It is not a fair win, because you chose it after seeing the held-out data.
  • Confirming it would take new photos that neither model has seen.

You earn the points for a real attempt, shown honestly, whether or not it does better.
