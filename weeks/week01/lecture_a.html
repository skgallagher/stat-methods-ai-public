Start with the model you know

\[Y_i\mid x_i\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_i),\qquad \operatorname{logit}(p_i)=\beta_0+x_i^T\beta.\]

This gives us three distinct pieces:

  • a probability model with an explicit feature set;
  • a likelihood and inferential assumptions;
  • a decision rule only after a threshold is chosen.

We use

\[\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},\qquad \operatorname{logit}(q)=\log\!\left\{\frac{q}{1-q}\right\}.\]

The Bernoulli likelihood is still underneath

For independent binary outcomes with a common probability \(p\) and \(k=\sum_i y_i\),

\[L(p)=p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad \ell(p)=k\log p+(n-k)\log(1-p).\]

Differentiate:

\[\ell'(p)=\frac{k}{p}-\frac{n-k}{1-p} =\frac{k-np}{p(1-p)}.\]

The stationary point solves \(k-np=0\).

The intercept-only MLE

Therefore \(\ell'(p)=0\) implies \(\widehat p=k/n\), and

\[\ell''(p)=-\frac{k}{p^2}-\frac{n-k}{(1-p)^2}<0\]

confirms the interior solution is a maximum. If \(p=\sigma(\beta_0)\), then \(\widehat\beta_0=\operatorname{logit}(k/n)\).

At the endpoints, \(k=0\) sends \(\widehat\beta_0\) toward \(-\infty\) and \(k=n\) sends it toward \(+\infty\); there is no finite intercept MLE.

Neural-network training will reuse this likelihood idea.

No hidden layer: exactly logistic regression

\[\widehat p_i=\sigma(w_0+x_i^Tw)\]

implies

\[\operatorname{logit}(\widehat p_i)=w_0+x_i^Tw.\]

The logit is one linear function of the original features: the same model class as logistic regression. For Bernoulli outcomes,

\[ -\ell(w_0,w) =-\sum_i\left\{y_i\log(\widehat p_i) +(1-y_i)\log(1-\widehat p_i)\right\} =\text{binary cross-entropy}. \]

With the same data, no penalty, and full optimization, this sigmoid network and logistic regression fit the same model.

Layer names are inconsistent across books. In this course, always say whether a hidden layer is present. “No hidden layer” is unambiguous; “one hidden layer” means the representation in a later slide.

Two equivalences—two ways to break them

  1. Model-class equivalence: the logit is one linear function of the original features. A nonlinear hidden layer breaks this statement.
  2. Fitting equivalence: minimizing binary cross-entropy reaches the same optimizer as maximizing the unpenalized Bernoulli likelihood. A penalty or early stopping can change the fitted coefficients without adding a hidden layer.

When claiming two procedures are “the same,” say whether you mean the functions they can represent or the estimate produced by a particular fitting procedure.

Model, estimate, metric, decision

These are different objects:

  • model: \(\widehat p(x)\);
  • estimand: population accuracy, sensitivity, or expected loss;
  • estimate: the value computed on evaluation data;
  • decision rule: classify, send for review, or collect more information.

Changing a threshold changes the decision rule, not the fitted model.

Derive a logistic-regression boundary

At threshold \(c\),

\[\widehat y=\mathbf 1\{\widehat p(x)\ge c\} =\mathbf 1\{\beta_0+x^T\beta\ge\operatorname{logit}(c)\},\]

because the sigmoid is increasing. Therefore the boundary satisfies

\[\beta_0+x^T\beta=\operatorname{logit}(c).\]

Changing \(c\) changes this decision boundary, not \(\widehat p(x)\).

Worked boundary: draw the line and the side

At \(c=0.5\), the boundary for

\[\widehat p(x)=\sigma(-0.6+1.5x_1-0.5x_2)\]

is

\[x_2=3x_1-1.2.\]

\((0,0)\) has score \(-0.6\); \((1,0)\) has score \(0.9\). The region below the line is classified as 1.

Parallel example: HW1 uses different coefficients.

The perceptron: same boundary algebra

\[\widehat y=\mathbf 1\{\beta_0+x^T\beta>0\}.\]

To find its boundary:

  1. set the linear score equal to zero;
  2. solve for one coordinate in terms of the other;
  3. substitute a point not on the line to determine the side classified as 1.

Multiplying every coefficient by a positive constant changes the score’s scale, but not the boundary or classifications.

Same linear score as logistic regression; fewer probability claims. What disappears when we retain only the classification boundary?

In-class transfer: use \(s(x)=2-x_1-2x_2\). Students should obtain \(x_2=1-x_1/2\), then check \((0,0)\) to determine that the region below the line is classified as 1. Ask what disappears relative to logistic regression: likelihood, calibrated probability interpretation, coefficient uncertainty, and threshold flexibility.

Basis expansion means choosing \(f(x)\)

Replace \(x\) with a specified transformation \(f(x)\):

\[ f(x)=\left(x_1,x_2,x_1^2,x_1x_2,x_2^2\right). \]

Then fit ordinary logistic regression on those transformed features:

\[\operatorname{logit}(p_i)=\beta_0+\beta^T f(x_i).\]

This is a quadratic model, not an arbitrary function.

The analyst chooses the transformation family—polynomials, splines, interactions, or something else. Fitting estimates coefficients within that family.

Neural networks retain this architectural choice; they parameterize the transformations differently.

One hidden layer, two hidden units

One layer may contain several units. Here the width is two:

\[ \begin{aligned} h_1(x)&=g(w_{10}+x^Tw_1),\\ h_2(x)&=g(w_{20}+x^Tw_2),\\ \widehat p(x)&=\sigma(\alpha_0+\alpha_1h_1+\alpha_2h_2). \end{aligned} \]

Set \(g(z)=z^2\) and simple weights, for example, and the hidden units can be \(x_1^2\) and \(x_2^2\).

You set the activation, width, and connectivity. Training learns weights within that specified family; the network is not free to represent “anything.”

Flexibility through bias and variance

At fixed \(x\), \(D\) is random training data and \(Y\) is an independent new response. The expected value averages over repeated \(D\) and the new \(Y\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[(Y-\widehat p_D(x))^2\mid x] &=\underbrace{p(x)(1-p(x))}_{\text{irreducible}}\\[0.25em] &\quad+\underbrace{\{\mathbb E[\widehat p_D(x)\mid x]-p(x)\}^2}_{\text{squared bias}}\\[0.25em] &\quad+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\{\widehat p_D(x)\mid x\}}_{\text{variance}}. \end{aligned} \]

HW1 derives this identity step by step. For today, read it as a warning: a richer specified family may reduce bias while the fitting procedure increases variance.

Notation note for class: subscripts on \(\mathbb E\) are common in machine-learning writing. We will state the source of randomness in words and use ordinary \(\mathbb E[\cdot]\). Bill Eddy’s terminology reminder was to say expected value, not expectation.

Worked comparison: lower bias can still lose

At one \(x\), suppose \(p(x)=0.40\). The irreducible term is \(0.40(0.60)=0.24\).

Procedure Mean fitted probability Variance Expected squared error
Logistic 0.35 0.010 \(0.24+0.05^2+0.010=0.2525\)
Flexible net 0.39 0.018 \(0.24+0.01^2+0.018=0.2581\)

The net has lower bias; logistic has lower expected squared error here. The comparison is about the entire fitting procedure under repeated training data, not flexibility in isolation.

Check: lower bias can still lose

At one \(x\), \(p(x)=0.50\). Procedure A has mean fitted probability \(0.45\) and variance \(0.010\); Procedure B has mean \(0.49\) and variance \(0.016\).

Compute the expected squared prediction error for each procedure. Which has lower bias? Which has lower expected error?

Flexibility creates an evaluation burden

A flexible system can find signal that a linear model misses. It can also exploit:

  • camera backgrounds;
  • duplicated sequences;
  • preprocessing artifacts;
  • label conventions;
  • an unrepresentative benchmark.

The more flexible the fitted function, the more consequential the study design.

Our image setting: Camera Traps

Client decision: can an AI system reduce expert sorting work without silently discarding animal images?

  • scored observation and accuracy denominator: image frame;
  • dependence: trigger sequence;
  • protected split and new-location target: camera location;
  • statistical baseline: logistic regression on documented image summaries;
  • AI system: supplied vision-model probabilities.

Our reported accuracy is pooled over frames, so represented cameras are weighted by their numbers of frames; it is not an equally weighted camera average.

Why keep the simple baseline?

The baseline tells us:

  • what brightness, color, edges, and night conditions already explain;
  • whether the AI adds meaningful predictive information;
  • whether both systems exploit the same shortcut;
  • how expensive flexibility is to justify.

It need not win to be useful.
