What, exactly, did our show of hands measure?

A fixed category can hide variable judgments

A dataset may record one category \(Y\). But if we repeat the measurement on the same item \(X=x\), the response can follow a distribution:

\[Y\mid X=x\sim\operatorname{Categorical}\{q_1(x),\ldots,q_K(x)\}.\]

The response probabilities depend on:

  • the item and its context;
  • the construct, question, and instructions;
  • the rater population or model.

An aggregation rule then turns a sample of responses into one reported category.

Important: this distribution describes judgments. A vote does not redefine factual truth or logical validity—but it does reveal a measurement problem.

DynaSent: five judgments per sentence

DynaSent is a benchmark for classifying English sentences as negative, neutral, or positive. For every sentence \(X=x\), it records

\[L_1,\ldots,L_n,\qquad n=5.\]

For a new judgment produced under the specified protocol, define the underlying response probabilities

\[q_k(x)=P(L^\star=k\mid X=x),\qquad k\in\{-,0,+\}.\]

This \(q(x)\) is the population quantity sometimes called the “true” proportion: it is true relative to the rater population and measurement protocol.

We do not observe \(q(x)\) directly.

[Sources] - Potts et al. (2021), DynaSent: https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.186/

Five observed labels estimate the response distribution

For class \(k\), turn judgment \(i\) into a zero-one indicator and average:

\[T_{ik}=\mathbf 1\{L_i=k\},\qquad \widehat q_k(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{ik},\quad n=5.\]

For example, five labels

\[(-,-,-,0,+)\]

give the empirical response proportions

\[\widehat q(x)=\left(\frac35,\frac15,\frac15\right).\]

Here \(q(x)\) is the underlying response distribution and \(\widehat q(x)\) is the observed five-rater distribution—an estimate, not the same quantity.

[Sources] - Potts et al. (2021), DynaSent: https://aclanthology.org/2021.acl-long.186/

First, hold the sentence fixed

Write response \(i\) as

\[Y_i=f_i(X,U_i),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,\]

Here \(X\) is the sentence; \(U_i\) is everything else that can vary for response \(i\)—rater, interpretation, attention, or model randomness.

\[X=x,\qquad Y_i=f_i(x,U_i).\]

Only the \(U_i\)’s vary. For two responses, the independence question is

\[Y_1\perp\!\!\!\perp Y_2\mid X=x\;?\]

Takeaway: Yes, under the stated model. If \(U_1\perp\!\!\!\perp U_2\) and no other shared input remains, then \(Y_1\perp\!\!\!\perp Y_2\mid X=x\). Fixing \(X\) removes only sentence-driven dependence.

Now let the sentence vary

Draw one sentence, then collect every response:

\[X\sim P_X,\qquad Y_i=f_i(X,U_i).\]

Now \(X\) varies too—and the same random \(X\) enters every response:

\[ Y_1=f_1(X,U_1),\qquad Y_2=f_2(X,U_2). \]

The marginal independence question is

\[Y_1\perp\!\!\!\perp Y_2\;?\]

Takeaway: Not necessarily. Conditional independence can become marginal dependence because both responses vary with the same \(X\). An ambiguous item, for example, can shift both response probabilities. Shared \(X\) permits—but does not guarantee—dependence.

Independence means factorization

Within a fixed sentence

\[ Y_1\perp\!\!\!\perp Y_2\mid X=x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{aligned} &P(Y_1=y_1,Y_2=y_2\mid X=x)\\ &=P(Y_1=y_1\mid X=x)P(Y_2=y_2\mid X=x) \end{aligned} \]

Across randomly drawn sentences

\[ Y_1\perp\!\!\!\perp Y_2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(Y_1=y_1,Y_2=y_2)=P(Y_1=y_1)P(Y_2=y_2). \]

These are two definitions for two different probability models. One factorization does not imply the other.

Covariance measures association

For a chosen class \(k\), use numeric indicators

\[Y_i=\mathbf 1\{L_i=k\},\qquad i=1,2.\]

Covariance measures whether two centered numeric responses tend to have the same sign:

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2) =E\!\left[(Y_1-E[Y_1])(Y_2-E[Y_2])\right]. \]

  • positive: the responses tend to be above or below their means together;
  • zero: the centered product has expected value zero;
  • negative: one tends to be above its mean when the other is below.

Independence implies zero covariance, but zero covariance is weaker than independence in general.

Center each response within and between sentences

To identify two sources of covariance, add and subtract the conditional mean:

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_1-E[Y_1] &=\underbrace{Y_1-E[Y_1\mid X]}_{\text{within-sentence residual}}\\ &\quad+\underbrace{E[Y_1\mid X]-E[Y_1]}_{\text{between-sentence shift}}. \end{aligned} \]

Likewise,

\[ \begin{aligned} Y_2-E[Y_2] &=\underbrace{Y_2-E[Y_2\mid X]}_{\text{within-sentence residual}}\\ &\quad+\underbrace{E[Y_2\mid X]-E[Y_2]}_{\text{between-sentence shift}}. \end{aligned} \]

Why the covariance decomposition works

Multiply the two centered-response decompositions and take expected values. The two cross-products disappear because conditional residuals have conditional expected value zero:

\[E[Y_i-E[Y_i\mid X]\mid X]=0,\qquad i=1,2.\]

What remains is within-sentence covariance + between-sentence covariance.

The law of total covariance keeps the questions straight

For two measurements \(Y_1\) and \(Y_2\) made on a shared random sentence \(X\),

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2) =\underbrace{E\!\left[\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2\mid X)\right]}_{\text{average within-sentence covariance}} +\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(E[Y_1\mid X],E[Y_2\mid X]\right)}_{\text{association across sentences}}. \]

The left side is marginal covariance over randomly drawn sentences. The two terms on the right describe two distinct sources of association.

[Sources] - Law of total covariance: https://statproofbook.github.io/P/cov-tot

Within-sentence covariance is zero under conditional independence

\[E[\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2\mid X)]\]

The term is exactly zero when the average conditional covariance is zero:

\[E[\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2\mid X)]=0.\]

A stronger, easier-to-interpret condition is

\[\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2\mid X=x)=0\quad\text{for every }x.\]

Conditional independence is sufficient for this:

\[Y_1\perp\!\!\!\perp Y_2\mid X,\]

but the reverse is not generally true: zero covariance is weaker than independence.

Within-sentence covariance can remain after fixing the sentence

\[E[\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2\mid X)]\ne0\]

requires systematic positive or negative covariance within fixed sentences.

For example:

  • one rater sees or discusses the other’s answer;
  • both raters receive the same extra hint for that item;
  • one reported response is constructed using the other response.

Takeaway: sentence difficulty cannot explain this within-sentence covariance because the sentence has already been held fixed. Look for another shared part of the measurement process.

Across-sentence covariance is zero when probabilities are unassociated

Because \(Y_i=\mathbf1\{L_i=k\}\), define

\[m_i(x)=E[Y_i\mid X=x]=P(L_i=k\mid X=x).\]

The across-sentence part is

\[\operatorname{Cov}(m_1(X),m_2(X)).\]

Concrete zero case: draw either sentence type with equal probability.

Sentence type \(X\) \(m_1(X)\) \(m_2(X)\)
clearly positive 0.9 0.5
clearly negative 0.1 0.5

Rater 1’s probability changes, but rater 2’s does not:

\[\operatorname{Cov}(m_1(X),m_2(X))=0.\]

More generally, this covariance is zero whenever the centered product averages to zero. Either \(m_i(x)\) being constant is an easy sufficient condition.

Across-sentence covariance is nonzero when probabilities change together

Draw one of two sentence types with equal probability. Two raters work independently conditional on the type.

Sentence type \(X\) \(m_1(X)\) \(m_2(X)\)
clearly positive 0.9 0.9
clearly negative 0.1 0.1

The total-covariance identity gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Within sentences:}\quad E[\operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2\mid X)] &=0,\\ \text{Across sentences:}\quad \operatorname{Cov}(m_1(X),m_2(X)) &=0.16,\\[2mm] \text{Total:}\quad \operatorname{Cov}(Y_1,Y_2)&=0+0.16=0.16. \end{aligned} \]

Within sentences, independence makes the first piece zero. Across sentences, the probabilities change together. Thus the responses are marginally dependent entirely because the sentence varies.

Frozen-model covariance comes from changing sentences

For a fixed sentence \(X=x\), the outputs \(f_1(x)\) and \(f_2(x)\) are constants:

\[\operatorname{Cov}(f_1(X),f_2(X)\mid X=x)=0.\]

For a random sentence \(X\), both outputs change with the sentence, so \(\operatorname{Cov}(f_1(X),f_2(X))\) can be nonzero.

Point: frozen models have no repeated-response randomness after \(X\) is fixed. Any covariance is across sentences.

Independent does not mean interchangeable

Independent: knowing rater 1’s answer does not change the distribution of rater 2’s answer.

Exchangeable: swapping the rater names does not change the joint distribution.

Fixed-sentence example Independent? Exchangeable?
Expert and novice work separately but have different response probabilities Yes No
Identical raters receive the same random cue No Yes

These are different modeling claims; neither implies the other.

Averaging labels does not erase dependence

The observed class-\(k\) proportion is

\[\widehat q_k=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^mT_{ik}.\]

Its conditional variance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\widehat q_k\mid X=x) =\frac{1}{m^2}\Bigg[&\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(T_{ik}\mid x)\\ &+2\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(T_{ik},T_{jk}\mid x)\Bigg]. \end{aligned} \]

Takeaway: positive covariance limits how much averaging reduces variance.

Conditional iid is what gives the \(1/m\) variance

For one class \(k\), let \(T_{ik}=\mathbf 1\{\text{rater }i\text{ chooses }k\}\).

The conditional iid model is

\[ T_{1k},\ldots,T_{mk}\mid X=x \ \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\ \operatorname{Bernoulli}\!\left(q_k(x)\right). \]

It gives

\[ E[\widehat q_k\mid x]=q_k(x),\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\widehat q_k\mid x) =\frac{q_k(x)(1-q_k(x))}{m}. \]

Point: the familiar \(1/m\) reduction is a consequence of the conditional iid model—not a consequence of averaging alone.

When we return: How should we score a probability forecast when labels can vary?

Why score the full probability forecast?

A model reports a distribution, not just a winning class:

\[p=(0.60,\ 0.30,\ 0.10),\]

  • Accuracy treats \(0.51\) and \(0.99\) alike when they predict the same class.
  • Confident mistakes should cost more than cautious mistakes.
  • We may compare the forecast with a hard label or observed rater proportions.

Brier score compares the full forecast with a chosen target.

Brier score is squared distance from the chosen target

Homework 2, Problem 1: you will derive the expected value of this score.

For DynaSent’s three classes, let \(p=(p_1,p_2,p_3)\) be the forecast and \(t=(t_1,t_2,t_3)\) the chosen target. Then

\[B(p,t)=\sum_{k=1}^3(p_k-t_k)^2.\]

\[B(p,t)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p=t.\] Larger values mean the forecast is farther from the target.

One realized score measures the total discrepancy. It cannot tell whether that discrepancy came from forecast mismatch, label variability, or how the target was constructed.

Expected Brier score separates the first two.

Expected Brier score exposes label variability

Fix a sentence \(X=x\). Let \(T\) be one random one-hot label and define

\[q_k(x)=P(T_k=1\mid X=x)=E[T_k\mid X=x].\]

For a fixed forecast \(p\),

\[ \begin{aligned} E[B(p,T)\mid x] &=\sum_k E[(p_k-T_k)^2\mid x]\\ &=\underbrace{\sum_k(p_k-q_k)^2}_{\text{forecast mismatch}} +\underbrace{\sum_k q_k(1-q_k)}_{\text{label variability}}. \end{aligned} \]

The cross term vanishes because \(E[T_k-q_k\mid x]=0\).

[Sources] - Bishop & Bishop, Deep Learning: Foundations and Concepts, Chapter 5 and Exercise 5.1.

Label variability is largest when responses are evenly split

\[\sum_k q_k(1-q_k)=1-\sum_k q_k^2.\]

Response distribution \(q\) Variability Meaning
\((1,0,0)\) \(0\) Every repeated label is the same.
\((0.8,0.2,0)\) \(0.32\) Labels sometimes disagree.
\((1/3,1/3,1/3)\) \(2/3\) Responses are evenly split.

Even when \(p=q\), the expected score is not generally zero: a newly generated label can still differ from the response distribution.

Averaging shrinks label variability by \(1/m\)

For \(\widehat q=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^mT_i\), the conditional iid model gives

\[ \begin{aligned} E[B(p,\widehat q)\mid x] &=\sum_k(p_k-q_k)^2 &&\text{forecast mismatch}\\[2mm] &\quad+\frac{1}{m}\sum_kq_k(1-q_k) &&\text{label variability}. \end{aligned} \]

Only the label-variability term shrinks; forecast mismatch is unchanged.

Dependence changes how much averaging buys us

Without conditional independence,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\widehat q_k\mid x) &=\frac{1}{m^2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(T_{ik}\mid x)\\ &\quad+\frac{2}{m^2}\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{Cov}(T_{ik},T_{jk}\mid x). \end{aligned} \]

Positive covariance leaves more variability in the average than the iid formula predicts. Negative covariance leaves less.

Therefore a soft-target Brier score is not “variability free.” Its expected variability depends on the rater design.

Hard and soft targets preserve different information

Five votes \((4,1,0)\) in negative, neutral, positive order give

\[t_{\text{hard}}=(1,0,0),\qquad t_{\text{soft}}=(0.8,0.2,0).\]

Hard target: keeps only the majority class; the neutral vote disappears.

Soft target: keeps all five vote fractions; it still reflects only five measurements.

Neither is literal ground truth. Comparing both scores shows how much the evaluation depends on the target choice.

What we did today

  1. Independence needs a repeated experiment: say what is held fixed and what is redrawn.
  2. Dependence has two sources: association within a sentence and across changing sentences.
  3. A shared sentence permits marginal dependence; it does not guarantee it.
  4. Expected Brier score separates forecast mismatch from label variability.
  5. The \(1/m\) reduction requires conditional iid raters. Dependence changes how much averaging helps.
