Week 2B: From Probability Model to Label Audit

Applying dependence and Brier-score ideas to DynaSent

Shannon Gallagher

Recap: the three claims from Lecture 2A

  1. A recorded category can hide a distribution of possible responses.
  2. Dependence means different things within a fixed sentence and across random sentences.
  3. Expected Brier score separates forecast mismatch from label variability.

Today we will repeat each idea with concrete sentences and numbers, then use the same workflow in the lab.

First hold one sentence fixed

For one fixed sentence:

“The service was fine.”

  • Repeat the measurement by drawing new raters.
  • The sentence stays fixed; rater responses vary.

Question: are responses independent conditional on this sentence?

Answer: not necessarily. Conditional independence is a modeling assumption; separate raters make it plausible, but a shared discussion, instructions, or context could make their responses dependent.

Then let the sentence change

Across a benchmark:

  • Draw a new sentence \(X\).
  • Collect multiple responses to that sentence.
  • Repeat with another sentence.

Question: are the responses independent across randomly sampled sentences?

Answer: not generally. Even if responses are independent conditional on each sentence, the shared random sentence \(X\) can make them marginally dependent.

Different repeated experiment, different independence claim.

Three categories create two kinds of dependence

For rater \(i\), write the one-hot response as

\[T_i=(T_{i-},T_{i0},T_{i+}),\qquad T_{i-}+T_{i0}+T_{i+}=1.\]

Between raters: for a fixed sentence, we might assume

\[T_1\perp T_2\mid X=x.\]

Within one rater: the three indicators cannot be independent. If \(q(x)=(.20,.50,.30)\), then

\[\operatorname{Cov}(T_{i0},T_{i+}\mid X=x)=0-(.50)(.30)=-.15.\]

Independent raters do not imply independent category indicators.

A changing sentence can create positive or negative dependence

Suppose A and B are equally likely and two raters are independent conditional on the sentence.

Sentence \(X\) \(q_0(X)\) \(q_+(X)\)
A: clear praise \(.15\) \(.80\)
B: mixed review \(.65\) \(.20\)

Across sentences,

\[\operatorname{Cov}(T_{1+},T_{2+})=\operatorname{Var}\{q_+(X)\}=.09,\]

while

\[\operatorname{Cov}(T_{10},T_{2+})=\operatorname{Cov}\{q_0(X),q_+(X)\}=-.075.\]

The sign depends on which response probabilities move together across \(X\).

Keep five votes attached to one sentence

For one DynaSent sentence \(j\):

“I waited only twenty minutes this time.”

\[ (L_{j1},\ldots,L_{j5})=(-,-,-,0,0) \]

\[ N_j=(3,2,0),\qquad \widehat q_j=(.60,.40,0),\qquad \text{majority}_j=-. \]

  • One sentence is the evaluation unit.
  • Five labels are its repeated measurements.
  • The vote counts, fractions, and majority are summaries of that same record.

The protocol changes what the label measures

Text:

“The camera is tiny, but the battery lasts all day.”

Protocol A: label the overall sentiment.

Protocol B: label mixed praise and criticism as neutral unless one side clearly dominates.

The text did not change. The measurement rule did—and the response distribution can change with it.

Before averaging votes, ask whether all raters received the same question, instructions, and context.

DynaSent has two item-construction processes

Round How sentences entered validation
1 Naturally occurring Yelp sentences selected because an initial model found them challenging
2 Workers edited Yelp prompts to fool a newer model trained using Round 1

Both rounds then used five-person validation.

Round 2 is not a randomized treatment applied to Round 1 sentences. A round comparison is descriptive: it compares two constructed item populations.

You will compare the rounds descriptively in the lab, then explain how their construction affects the target in Homework 2, Problem 2.

Keep the five votes visible during EDA

For each sentence, compute

\[N_k=\sum_{i=1}^5T_{ik},\qquad \widehat q_k=N_k/5.\]

Votes \((N_-,N_0,N_+)\) Majority What the majority hides
\((5,0,0)\) negative unanimous judgment
\((3,2,0)\) negative a nearby neutral boundary
\((3,0,2)\) negative polarized sentiment judgments

All three have the same majority class. They are not the same measurement outcome.

In the lab, these patterns guide which sentences you inspect closely.

Read a 3–2 split with the sentence

Suppose the votes are \((3,2,0)\) for

“I waited only twenty minutes this time.”

The count tells us that raters disagreed. The sentence can suggest why:

  • “only” may signal improvement;
  • twenty minutes may still be a complaint;
  • “this time” invokes missing prior context.

This is purposeful case inspection. It suggests mechanisms; it does not estimate how common each mechanism is.

Worked Brier example: hold the forecast fixed

Use class order negative, neutral, positive. Five votes are

\[N=(0,2,3),\]

and the model forecast is

\[p=(0.10,0.35,0.55).\]

The same measurements produce two targets:

\[t_{\text{hard}}=(0,0,1),\qquad t_{\text{soft}}=(0,0.40,0.60).\]

Next: score the same forecast against each target.

Against the hard target, the score is \(0.335\)

\[ \begin{aligned} B(p,t_{\text{hard}}) &=(.10-0)^2+(.35-0)^2+(.55-1)^2\\ &=.010+.1225+.2025\\ &=0.335. \end{aligned} \]

The majority rule turns the target into “positive with probability 1.” It discards both neutral votes.

Against the soft target, the score is \(0.015\)

\[ \begin{aligned} B(p,t_{\text{soft}}) &=(.10-0)^2+(.35-.40)^2+(.55-.60)^2\\ &=.010+.0025+.0025\\ &=0.015. \end{aligned} \]

The forecast did not change. The soft target preserved the observed \(2/5\)–\(3/5\) split.

What did the lower Brier score mean?

It did not show that the model suddenly improved.

It showed that the model forecast was much closer to the empirical vote distribution than to the one-hot majority target.

Report:

  1. what information the hard target discarded;
  2. how many measurements formed the soft target;
  3. whether conclusions about model performance changed.

Neither target is automatically “the truth.”

The lab repeats this comparison on the data; Homework 2, Problem 1 explains its expected value.

Align the forecasters by sentence

For every sampled sentence \(X_j\), record all three forecasts on the same row:

Sentence Model A Model B Student
\(X_1\): clear praise \(p_A(X_1)\) \(p_B(X_1)\) \(p_S(X_1)\)
\(X_2\): mixed review \(p_A(X_2)\) \(p_B(X_2)\) \(p_S(X_2)\)
\(\vdots\) \(\vdots\) \(\vdots\) \(\vdots\)

The rows may be iid if the sentences \(X_1,X_2,\ldots\) are sampled iid and each forecasting procedure stays fixed.

The three entries within a row are not three iid rater draws:

  • not identically distributed: A, B, and the student use different procedures;
  • not justified as independent: all three forecasts are functions of the same sentence \(X_j\).

They are paired comparisons on each sentence. Homework 2, Problem 3 asks you to use this structure with actual forecasts.
