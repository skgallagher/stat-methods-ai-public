Question 1: what does a confidence of 0.9 claim?

The team’s next decision

In Lab 5 and Homework 5, a team chose a language model for a tool that answers reasoning questions. Now it wants a version small enough to run on a phone, and it is looking at Gemma-2-2B. Its plan:

  • when the model is confident, the tool answers on its own;
  • when it is not, a person answers the question.

The model’s answer is its most likely option. Its confidence is that option’s probability, after the option probabilities are rescaled to add to 1. (The model also gives some probability to text that isn’t an option.) With K options, the confidence is at least 1/K.

The plan is safe only if a confidence of 0.9 means the answer is right about 90% of the time.

About 2 minutes. Most classifiers report confidence this way: the probability of the answer they chose. The rescaling matters: Llama, for example, puts a median of only about 15% of its probability on the listed options. These are token probabilities, not a confidence the model writes in words; Thursday covers the difference.

A confidence is a claim about many questions

One answer with confidence 0.9 cannot show anything: it is either right or wrong. The claim is about all the questions that get confidence 0.9. A model is calibrated if

P(\text{answer is right} \mid \text{confidence} = c) = c \qquad \text{for every } c.

Among questions answered with confidence 0.9, about 90% of the answers should be right. Among questions answered with confidence 0.6, about 60% should be.

This is the frequentist reading of probability: 0.9 is a long-run frequency over many similar answers. So we check it by counting outcomes on questions with known answers, without knowing how the model works.

A Bayesian reads 0.9 as a degree of belief about one question, which no single outcome can refute. Even so, Dawid (1982) showed that a coherent Bayesian expects their own forecasts to be calibrated in the long run, so a calibration check tests a Bayesian’s model too. Little (2006) calls this “calibrated Bayes.”

About 4 minutes. Write the conditional probability on the board. Because the claim is about pairs of a confidence and a right-or-wrong outcome, a statistician can check a closed model from its outputs and the outcomes alone. Ask: what data would you need? Questions with known answers, from the population where the tool will be used.

On the footnote, if asked: for a Bayesian, a probability is a degree of belief, so “0.9” for one question is not a frequency claim. But Dawid showed that a coherent Bayesian assigns prior probability one to being well calibrated over a long sequence of their own forecasts. If the forecasts turn out badly calibrated, the forecaster’s model of the world was wrong. Rubin argued that applied Bayesians should check their models with frequency calculations like this one, and Little calls the combination, Bayesian inference with frequentist checks of the model, “calibrated Bayes.”

References: Dawid, A. P. (1982), “The Well-Calibrated Bayesian,” Journal of the American Statistical Association 77, 605–610. Rubin, D. B. (1984), “Bayesianly Justifiable and Relevant Frequency Calculations for the Applied Statistician,” Annals of Statistics 12, 1151–1172. Little, R. J. (2006), “Calibrated Bayes: A Bayes/Frequentist Roadmap,” The American Statistician 60, 213–223.

Discuss: before concluding that Gemma’s confidence is too high, what would you check?

About 4 minutes. Take answers. Ones to draw out:

  • The scoring rule. The confidence comes from one prompt format with three worked examples, and here the answer is the most likely option without the leaderboard’s length adjustment. For Gemma, right and wrong under this rule match Lab 5’s data on 88% of questions.
  • The tasks. The 5,761 questions come in 24 tasks (Lab 5), so they are not 5,761 independent pieces of evidence.
  • The population. BBH is a public benchmark of hard tasks. The team’s users will ask their own questions, and calibration on BBH need not carry over.
  • The answers. Some BBH tasks compute the right answer by rule (Boolean expressions, navigation, object tracking); others were labeled by people and may contain mistakes. Worth a look, but label errors are unlikely to explain a 33-point gap.

None of these is likely to turn 0.76 into 0.43. Verified: accuracy 2,453 / 5,761 = 0.426; average confidence 0.761.

The reliability diagram

Points below the diagonal are overconfident, and points above it are underconfident. When a bin’s interval crosses the diagonal, that bin’s gap could be chance. The numbers are the questions in each bin; always show them.

About 3 minutes. Two things are in this one picture. The points sit below the diagonal, so the probabilities are too high. But they rise from left to right, so a more confident answer really is more likely to be right. Thursday uses both facts. Most published reliability diagrams show neither intervals nor counts.

Guo et al. (2017), this week’s reading, found the same shape for modern image classifiers.

Check

On the team’s date questions, 150 answers have average confidence 0.90, and 120 of them are right. The 95% Wilson interval for accuracy is [0.73, 0.86].

Is the model overconfident on these questions? Could the gap be chance?

Accuracy is 120/150 = 0.80, which is 10 points below the stated 0.90. The interval does not contain 0.90, so the model is overconfident on date questions, and the gap is not chance.

A model can be close to calibrated over all questions and still be overconfident for one group, such as one kind of question.

About 3 minutes. Wilson for 120/150: [0.729, 0.856]. The last sentence sets up “What ECE hides” and matches the midterm review packet, Problem 1, item 8.

Accuracy and calibration answer different questions

Accurate but overconfident. A model’s answers are right 90% of the time, but it says 0.99 on every one. The answers are good, and the confidence is not.

Calibrated but useless. On yes-or-no questions, a model is 50% sure of every answer and right on half of them. That is perfectly calibrated, and it has told the team nothing.

A useful model has to be calibrated, and it also has to separate easy questions from hard ones, saying 0.99 for some and 0.6 for others. Calibration is necessary, but it is not enough.

Morris DeGroot and Stephen Fienberg, both of CMU Statistics, made this distinction precise for weather forecasters in 1983.

About 3 minutes. Weather forecasters have published probability forecasts and been scored on them since the 1950s, and rain forecasts are close to calibrated. The “always 50%” model is the calibration version of Week 1’s floor. Reference: DeGroot and Fienberg (1983), “The Comparison and Evaluation of Forecasters,” The Statistician 32, 12–22.

Question 2: one number, and what it hides

Expected calibration error

The expected calibration error (ECE) averages the bins’ gaps, weighting each bin by its number of questions:

\text{ECE} = \sum_{b} \frac{n_b}{n}\,\bigl|\,\text{accuracy}_b - \text{confidence}_b\,\bigr|.

For Gemma, every bin’s accuracy is below its average confidence, so every term has the same sign. Then ECE is just average confidence minus accuracy:

\text{ECE} = 0.761 - 0.426 = 0.335.

On an average question, Gemma’s confidence is off by about 34 percentage points.

About 3 minutes. Show the shortcut on the board: when all gaps are positive, the weighted average of (confidence − accuracy) over bins is the overall average confidence minus the overall accuracy. The script value with ten bins is 0.335. ECE is in percentage points, which a client can follow. This week’s reading, Guo et al. (2017), uses 15 equal-width bins.

What ECE hides

ECE is one number for a whole diagram, so it leaves things out.

  1. It depends on the bins. Ten bins and fifteen bins give different ECEs on the same questions. Report the bins you used.
  2. It drops the direction. An ECE of 0.04 could come from overconfidence, underconfidence, or some of each.
  3. Gaps in opposite directions can cancel in the overall averages. The shortcut on the last slide works only when every gap has the same sign. When the gaps have different signs, the overall averages can match while the bins are far off.
  4. It averages over groups. A small ECE over all questions can hide one task where the model is 30 points overconfident.

So report the diagram, with its counts, next to the number.

About 4 minutes. Example for point 3: two bins of equal size, one 10 points overconfident and one 10 points underconfident. Average confidence equals accuracy, but ECE is 0.10. Thursday’s “grade this claim” uses this.

One more point if there is time: ECE is itself an estimate, and it is biased upward. With small bins, even a perfectly calibrated model shows a positive ECE, because the absolute value of noise is positive.

The Brier score from Week 2

For one question, let Y = 1 if the answer is right and x be the stated confidence. The Brier score is (x - Y)^2. In Week 2 we showed that if the true probability is q,

E\big[(x - Y)^2\big] = \underbrace{(x - q)^2}_{\text{forecast mismatch}} + \underbrace{q(1 - q)}_{\text{outcome variability}}.

The expected score is smallest at x = q, so the honest probability gets the best score. With q = 0.7:

Stated x 0.7 (honest) 0.9 (overconfident) 0.5 (hedging)
Expected Brier score 0.21 0.25 0.25

Accuracy does not reward honest probabilities: any x above 0.5 gives the same answer, so it gets the same accuracy. Models get better at whatever they are graded on.

About 4 minutes. A score that is best at the honest probability is called proper. Weather forecasters have been graded on the Brier score since 1950, and leaderboards mostly grade accuracy. Ask which group you would expect to be better calibrated.

The Brier score mixes calibration with how well the model separates easy questions from hard ones. A lower Brier score alone does not say which of the two improved, so report it with the diagram.

Question 3: when can the model answer on its own?

A rule built on confidence

The rule: the tool answers on its own when the model’s confidence is at least t. A person answers every other question.

Two numbers describe the rule:

\text{share answered on its own} = \frac{\text{questions with confidence} \ge t}{\text{all questions}},

\text{error rate among those questions} = \frac{\text{wrong answers with confidence} \ge t}{\text{questions with confidence} \ge t}.

The error rate’s denominator is the questions the tool answered, not all questions. Raising t lowers both numbers: the tool answers fewer questions and gets fewer of them wrong.

About 3 minutes. Say the denominator twice. Dividing by all questions is the most common mistake on this kind of question. In ML papers the two numbers are called coverage and selective risk.

Gemma’s rule at a threshold of 0.90

Gemma answers 2,260 of the 5,761 questions with confidence of at least 0.9.

  • The tool would answer 2{,}260 / 5{,}761 = 39\% of questions on its own.
  • Their average confidence is 0.97, so Gemma promises about 3% wrong answers.
  • The data show 945 / 2{,}260 = 41.8\% wrong, with 95% Wilson interval [39.8%, 43.9%].

The team will accept at most 10% wrong. What to tell them:

At a threshold of 0.90, the tool would answer about 39% of BBH questions on its own, and about 42% of those answers would be wrong (95% interval 40% to 44%). Gemma’s confidence promises about 3%, so it is badly overconfident here, and the tool would be far over your 10% limit. Even at 0.99, 30% of its answers are wrong.

About 5 minutes. Two separate questions are answered here: whether the confidence holds up (it does not: 3% promised, 42% delivered) and whether the client’s limit is met (no). The midterm review packet, Problem 2, part 5, asks the same two questions about complaint routing.

At 0.99: 680 questions (12%), 205 wrong, 30.1%, Wilson [26.8%, 33.7%]. The client sentence has the same three parts as Week 4’s: an estimate, an interval, and what it means for the decision. And it is about BBH; the team’s own questions could differ.

Check

A better-calibrated model answers 200 of 1,000 questions with confidence of at least 0.97. Their average confidence is 0.98, and 4 answers are wrong. The 95% Wilson interval for the error rate is [0.8%, 5.0%].

  1. Does the model’s confidence hold up on these questions?
  2. Can the team promise at most 5% wrong answers at this threshold?
  1. The model promises about 2% wrong answers, and it gets 4/200 = 2.0\% wrong. Here its confidence holds up.
  2. Not quite. The interval reaches 5.0%, so the data are consistent with an error rate right at the limit. More questions with known answers above 0.97 could settle it.

About 3 minutes. Wilson for 4/200: [0.0078, 0.0503]. Students should compare the interval with the limit, not the point estimate. Point out the pattern: the higher the threshold, the fewer questions the estimate rests on, so the interval is widest where the promise is strongest. That is Week 4’s lesson that better systems are harder to verify.

What we did today

  1. A confidence is a claim about many cases: among cases with confidence c, about a fraction c of the answers should be right. One case cannot check it.
  2. Each bin’s accuracy is an estimated proportion. A reliability diagram with Wilson intervals and counts shows which gaps are real.
  3. Accuracy and calibration are different. A calibrated model is useless if it never separates easy cases from hard ones.
  4. ECE summarizes the diagram in percentage points, but it depends on the bins, drops the direction, and averages over groups. The Brier score rewards honest probabilities.
  5. A rule that lets the model answer when confident is described by the share of cases it answers and the error rate among them. That error rate is an estimate, so compare its interval with the client’s limit.
  6. Calibration belongs to a model and a population together.
