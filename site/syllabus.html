Course description

Modern AI systems arrive with benchmark scores, probability claims, and confident recommendations. This course asks what those numbers actually mean.

Students will study AI systems through a statistician’s perspective. They will define populations and estimands, investigate how data and labels were produced, assess the quality and assumptions of benchmarks, quantify uncertainty, compare systems fairly, test consequential assumptions, and interpret results in context.

This class is not a survey of machine-learning algorithms but rather a study of how a statistician or data scientist should use these systems. In this course, students will both use statistical thinking to evaluate AI systems and use AI to enhance their statistical output, thus creating a feedback loop where one strategy can propel the other forward.

Course goals

The course has two linked goals:

  1. Develop statisticians who can evaluate empirical claims about AI systems.
  2. Develop statisticians who can use AI tools productively without outsourcing statistical judgment.

Every substantial analysis follows the same five-stage workflow:

  1. Define the research question, hypotheses, and estimands.
  2. Explore the data and assess its quality.
  3. Model the data, make inferences, and evaluate performance.
  4. Test how sensitive the conclusions are to key assumptions.
  5. Make a decision and state its limitations and next steps.

Learning objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Translate a claim about an AI system into a statistical question with a target population, unit, and estimand.
  2. Evaluate how data and labels were produced and how that shapes what a benchmark measures.
  3. Quantify uncertainty in AI performance and compare systems fairly against a statistical baseline.
  4. Judge whether a system’s probability claims are calibrated and whether its performance carries over to new populations.
  5. Use AI tools in statistical work while checking and documenting what they contribute.
  6. Make and defend a recommendation whose strength matches the evidence.

Audience and prerequisites

The course is for advanced undergraduates and master’s students. The prerequisite is 36-401 (Modern Regression) or an equivalent course. Students should be comfortable with:

  • interpreting regression coefficients and fitted probabilities;
  • confidence intervals and hypothesis tests;
  • basic data manipulation, visualization, and programming;
  • explaining a statistical result in writing.

Further coursework in statistics or machine learning helps but is not required. Machine learning is not the point of this course. We study AI systems only as far as we need to evaluate them, and Week 1 builds up to neural networks starting from regression.

Course format

Weeks 1–7

Tuesdays usually introduce a statistical problem and work through examples. Thursdays usually pair a shorter lecture with a hands-on lab. A short online checkpoint quiz closes out each week.

Weeks 9–15

After spring break, the course shifts to the team project. One class each week introduces a new statistical topic. The other is mostly a project work session, where teams work together in class.

Final project

The final project is the center of the course. In teams of four, students act as statistical consultants. A client has an AI system, some data, and a decision to make, and the team designs and carries out a statistical study to inform that decision. Every team fits a statistical baseline it can explain, compares it fairly with the AI system, quantifies uncertainty, tests whether its conclusion survives a reasonable alternative analysis, and makes a recommendation that matches the evidence.

Teams choose one of four tracks:

Track Client question Data and AI system
Food tagging Should an online marketplace tag food photos automatically? Food-101 images; CLIP image classifier
Pet tagging Can the system tag pet breeds, or only cats versus dogs? Oxford-IIIT Pet images; CLIP image classifier
Human preferences Does an LLM judge measure what people actually prefer? Stanford Human Preferences pairs from Reddit; LLM judge
Instruction following Is an LLM judge a trustworthy way to check whether responses follow instructions? IFEval-style prompts and responses; rule checker and LLM judge

Data, cached AI outputs, and starter code are provided for every track. Teams and track choices are set by the midterm. The project then runs through the end of the semester, with two milestones, a short ending artifact, a recorded video, an oral defense, and a written report.

Major topics

  • From regression to neural networks
  • Data and labels as measurements
  • Evaluation design, including estimands, splits, and leakage
  • Uncertainty in benchmark performance
  • Comparing systems on the same cases
  • Calibration and decision rules
  • Distribution shift and sensitivity analysis
  • Designed evaluations and LLM judges

Data and computing

Labs and homework use a few datasets that recur through the semester, plus others when a topic calls for it. The final project uses the track datasets described above.

We use Python in Google Colab on free, CPU-only resources. Data subsets, starter code, and cached AI-system outputs are provided, so no assignment requires a paid service, a GPU, or a large download. Rules for shared lab notebooks, service outages, and typed versus handwritten work are on the course technology page in Canvas.

Do not upload course data or project materials to an outside service, including an AI tool, unless the assignment says you may.

Homework

There are seven graded homeworks, and the lowest score is dropped. Homework is usually due Tuesday at 11:59 pm. Project milestones are separate from homework.

Each homework has three problems. Problem 1 is a derivation or hand calculation done without AI, and it is good practice for the midterm. Problem 2 is a data analysis that picks up where the lab left off. Problem 3 asks you to use an AI tool and then check and correct what it produced.

You may reuse code from lab. The analysis and the writing you turn in must be your own.

You do all homework in a Colab notebook that we provide. Submit two files to Gradescope, a PDF of the finished notebook and the notebook itself. Handwritten work is welcome but never required. You can type math in the notebook, or insert a photo of handwritten work followed by a typed sentence stating your conclusion.

Before HW1, you will complete HW0. It is an ungraded practice run of the whole process, so submission problems get fixed before anything counts.

Labs and project work sessions

A lab is a guided coding session in class. The instructor works through an example in a Colab notebook. Then students change the code, run it, and talk through the results with classmates. Labs vary from week to week, but most are set up so that the homework starts where the lab leaves off and reuses its code.

Labs are not graded. Skipping one will usually make that week’s homework harder.

After spring break, Thursdays are mostly project work sessions. Teams work on their projects in class, often after a short guided exercise that builds part of the next milestone. This work is not graded separately.

Weekly checkpoints

Each week ends with a short online checkpoint quiz in Canvas. It opens after Thursday’s class, covers that week’s lectures and lab, and is due the following Tuesday at 11:59 pm. Once you open it, you have 20 minutes to finish, and you get one attempt.

Checkpoints are individual. You may use your own notes, but not AI tools, classmates, or any other outside help. Questions come directly from that week’s lectures, so if you followed lecture and reviewed your notes, a checkpoint should not take long. They are also good practice for the midterm.

There are ten checkpoints, one for each of Weeks 1–6 and 9–12. Your lowest two scores are dropped. That covers ordinary illness, travel, and busy weeks, so there are no makeups or extensions. If something serious affects more than two checkpoints, contact the instructor.

If attendance starts to drop, I will move checkpoints into class time.

Midterm

The midterm is in class on Thursday, March 4, and is designed to be challenging. It is completed individually without AI tools, networked devices, or books. You may bring one sheet of notes you prepared yourself, front and back (US letter or A4, handwritten or typed). There is no final exam.

Final project

Each team carries out a full statistical evaluation of one AI system for a client, following the five steps above. Every project includes a simple reference point (such as always predicting the most common class) and a statistical baseline the team fits and can explain, usually logistic regression. It also includes uncertainty for each key estimate, a fair comparison between the baseline and the AI system, one sensitivity analysis that could change the conclusion, and a recommendation that matches the evidence.

The project is 40% of the course grade, divided as follows:

Deliverable Share of project grade
Milestone 1: questions, estimands, EDA, and design 15%
Milestone 2: modeling, inference, and evaluation 20%
Ending artifact: sensitivity analysis and decision 5% (completion)
Five-minute recorded video 20%
Oral discussion and defense 20%
Written report 10%
Within-team peer assessment 10%

The video is the polished, client-facing argument. The oral defense checks that every team member understands it. The ending artifact is a small completion grade meant to keep teams from starting the final argument in the last week.

For the peer assessment, each student privately evaluates their own teammates, with specific examples. Ratings are not averaged mechanically, and documented, substantial non-contribution can lower an individual’s project grade.

Course policies

Attendance and participation

Regular attendance is strongly encouraged. Labs and project work sessions are hard to make up from slides alone, and they are where homework and project work usually start. Attendance is not graded. If attendance drops, the weekly checkpoint quizzes will move into class time.

If illness, a religious observance, university travel, disability-related needs, or an emergency prevents attendance, notify course staff as soon as reasonably possible. Students are not expected to disclose private medical details. We will identify an appropriate way to recover the essential learning or artifact.

Late work

There are no flex days. If you need more time on an assignment, ask the instructor before the deadline. Extensions of up to 24 hours are considered case by case.

Weekly checkpoints do not get extensions, because the lowest two scores are already dropped. University-approved accommodations and serious emergencies are handled individually.

Work submitted late without an approved extension may receive reduced or no credit.

Collaboration

Statistical work benefits from discussion, but collaboration must remain visible and must not replace individual understanding.

  • Labs. Work with whomever you like. Talking through the lab with classmates is encouraged.
  • Homework. You may discuss concepts, debugging, and the lab code with classmates. You must run the analysis, make the decisions, and write the code and prose yourself. Do not share finished answers, solution files, or text or code that others could copy.
  • Weekly checkpoints. These are individual. Your own notes are allowed, but not AI tools, classmates, or other outside help.
  • Midterm. No collaboration or outside help.
  • Project. Collaborate within your team. Sharing project work outside your team requires instructor permission.

Every individual submission must list collaborators and other assistance, or state that none was received. Every external source, including borrowed code and AI assistance, must be acknowledged where required.

AI tools

AI is part of how this course is taught, and two rules apply whenever you use it. You are responsible for every statement, number, citation, and line of code you turn in. You must also record what the AI contributed and how you checked it, so another analyst could follow what happened.

AI is allowed, and sometimes required, on homework and the project. Each assignment says how. Sometimes you will use AI to explain an idea or draft code, and sometimes the AI system is the thing you are evaluating. Making up citations, turning in output you didn’t check, or passing off AI work as your own is not acceptable.

AI is not allowed on weekly checkpoints or the midterm. “The AI said so” is never statistical evidence.

AI built into other tools counts too. That includes the Colab assistant, chat panels in editors, and autocomplete that writes code or prose for you. Turn these off when an assignment or problem says no AI. Don’t give them course data unless the assignment says you may.

Academic integrity

Honesty, attribution, and transparency are part of the statistical practice this course teaches. Students must comply with Carnegie Mellon’s Policy on Academic Integrity. The policy requires original work and acknowledgement of permitted collaboration and assistance. In this course, that acknowledgement includes people, sources, software, and AI tools as described above.

Copying another student’s work, providing copyable individual solutions, using unauthorized assistance, fabricating data or citations, misrepresenting an AI-generated contribution, or using prohibited tools on an assessment may constitute an academic-integrity violation. Ask before submitting if the boundary is unclear.

Regrade requests

Requests to correct a grading or scoring error must be submitted in writing within seven calendar days of the graded work being returned. The request should identify the relevant rubric criterion and explain the suspected error. A regrade reviews the relevant work for accuracy and consistency; it is not a negotiation for additional points. Clerical errors should be reported at any time.

Devices and recording

You will need a laptop for labs and some class activities. During discussion, I may ask you to close it.

Do not record class (audio, video, photos, or screenshots) without my written permission or an approved disability accommodation. Any approved recording is for your own use and may not be shared. This protects class discussion, student work, and guest speakers.

If course technology goes down

If Colab, Canvas, or another course service goes down, save what you can, note when it happened, and keep working on anything that doesn’t need it. Don’t pay for a substitute or move course data somewhere else. If a service is down for more than 15 minutes during class, we will finish the lab another time. If a confirmed outage hits in the last six hours before a homework or project deadline, everyone gets an extension, announced on Canvas. If your own computer or internet fails, tell us right away and we will help you find another way to work or submit.

Respect, inclusion, and religious observances

Different experiences and perspectives are an asset in a course about measurement, populations, human judgments, and consequential decisions. We will critique claims and methods rigorously while treating classmates and the people represented in data with care. Students should use one another’s stated names and pronouns and make room for equitable participation in group work and discussion.

Students who experience a conflict with a religious or cultural observance should contact the instructor as early as practical. The course calendar will be checked against CMU’s inclusive holiday and interfaith calendar when dates are finalized.

How to succeed in this course

  • Treat every headline number as an estimate. Name its target population and uncertainty.
  • Keep a running analysis notebook and AI-use log rather than reconstructing decisions at the deadline.
  • Finish each lab. It usually sets up the homework.
  • Review your lecture notes before each checkpoint, and do homework Problem 1 without AI. Both prepare you for the midterm.
  • Read for a purpose. Identify the figure, assumption, or documentation detail the week’s analysis uses.
  • Start project questions with the client decision, not with an available metric.
  • Use office hours before a small design ambiguity becomes a large coding problem.
  • Be prepared to explain any submitted result without relying on the AI tool, teammate, or code that produced it.
